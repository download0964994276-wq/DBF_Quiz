Task 1: Does the claim show a cause?

The data show a strong positive association: banks with more collection staff have more overdue loans. But association is not causation, so the report's conclusion, "Hiring collection staff causes overdue loans," does not follow.

1. Confounding Variable: Bank Size (Total Loans)

Total loans rise alongside collection staff: Bank A has 40 million in loans and 4 staff, while Bank F has 160 million and 24 staff. Size drives both variables, so staff and overdue loans move together.

Bank E181405.6M

Currently viewing raw overdue amounts. Notice how larger banks have higher absolute numbers.

2. Reverse Causality

Causation could run the other way: banks with more overdue loans respond by hiring more collection staff. The overdue loans came first, and the hiring was the reaction.

Cautious Rewrite: "Across these six banks at year-end, banks with more collection staff also tended to have more overdue loans. This may largely reflect differences in bank size, and the data cannot show that hiring staff causes overdue loans."

Task 2: Which bank approves more applications?

An exploration of Simpson's Paradox where individual group success rates reverse when aggregated due to differing applicant mixes.

Risk Group Bank R Approval Rate Bank S Approval Rate
Lower risk90% (72/80)95% (19/20)
Higher risk40% (8/20)50% (40/80)
Overall Rate80% (80/100)59% (59/100)

Applicant Mix & Standardization

Bank R's pool is 80% lower-risk, whereas Bank S's pool is only 20% lower-risk. Because lower-risk applicants are approved at much higher rates, Bank R's overall average is heavily pulled up.

Natural Mix

Bank R: 65% weighted average.
Bank S: 72.5% with identical 50/50 mix.

Fair One-Sentence Report

"Bank S approves a higher share than Bank R within both risk groups, but Bank R's overall rate is higher (80% vs 59%) because 80% of its applicants are lower-risk compared with 20% at Bank S; with an identical 50/50 mix, S would be ahead (72.5% vs 65%)."

Task 3: Who is Missing?

Analyzing sampling and selection biases across various institutional data collection methods.

Case Data Source Bias Type Missing / Overrepresented Practical Improvement
A Survey via smartphone app Selection (coverage) bias Missing: older, rural, app-nonusers. Over: digital-savvy. Add SMS, phone, paper; randomly sample full list.
B Student interviews near campus Convenience sampling bias Missing: other branch customers. Over: students near campus. Randomly select branches stratified by region.
C Only surviving banks after 10 years Survivorship bias Missing failed/merged banks. Winners only. Include all baseline banks and track exits.
D Records with missing income dropped Missing-data non-response bias Missing informal workers. Over: formal employees. Handle missing values properly via imputation.

Sample Size Fallacy: Would ten times as many records from the same flawed process remove these biases? No. A larger sample from a flawed process just gives a more precise estimate of the same biased answer.

Optional Extensions

A. Training the Worst Branches

Selecting branches based on extreme high complaint rates guarantees that random luck played a part. Consequently, their scores naturally drift downward next month even without intervention (regression to the mean).

B. Provinces and Households

The Ecological Fallacy: Drawing a conclusion about individual households from group (province-level) averages. Provinces with higher average income having higher average debt does not mean richer households within a province borrow more. The household-level pattern could be completely different or even reversed.
